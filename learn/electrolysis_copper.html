Experiment guide

Copper Sulfate Electrolysis

Electrorefining/electroplating cell: copper electrodes in CuSO₄ solution. Cu²⁺ deposits at the cathode; the copper anode dissolves to replenish the solution, so copper is transferred from anode to cathode while the bath concentration stays roughly constant.

1. Principle

Idea. Passing a DC current through CuSO₄ solution between two copper electrodes drives two half-reactions: at the cathode, Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and plate out as solid copper (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu); at the anode, copper metal is oxidized and dissolves into solution (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻). Because the same amount of copper leaves the anode as deposits on the cathode, the CuSO₄ concentration in the bath stays essentially unchanged — this is the basis of copper electrorefining and electroplating.

2. Key formulas

n = \dfrac{Q}{zF} = \dfrac{It}{zF}\quad\cdot\quad m = \dfrac{I t M}{zF}
  • n = moles of copper deposited, Q = charge (C), I = current (A), t = time (s).
  • z = 2 electrons transferred per Cu²⁺ ion, F = 96485 C/mol (Faraday constant).
  • m = mass deposited (g), M = 63.55 g/mol (molar mass of copper).
  • Ask which quantity you control in the simulator (I, t), and which you measure (mass deposited).

3. What to observe

In the sim. Blue Cu²⁺ ions drift from the anode toward the cathode. The reddish copper deposit layer on the cathode thickens with elapsed time while the anode very slightly thins as it dissolves. The bottom plot traces mass deposited vs time — a straight line, since Faraday's law says mass is directly proportional to charge (and hence to time at fixed current).

4. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Start from the two half-reactions: reduction at the cathode, oxidation at the copper anode.
Step B. Write the governing relation: m = \dfrac{I t M}{zF}
Step C. Change current or time in the experiment and predict the sign and size of the change in deposited mass before you look.
Step D. Note why the anode is made of copper (not an inert electrode) in this scheme: it keeps replenishing Cu²⁺ in solution, so the process can run indefinitely without depleting the bath — the essence of electrorefining/electroplating.
