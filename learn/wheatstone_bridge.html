Wheatstone Bridge

Four resistors in a diamond, a battery across one diagonal, a galvanometer across the other: balance the bridge to measure an unknown resistance with high precision.

1. Principle

Idea. R1 and R2 form the top two arms, R3 and Rx the bottom two arms, meeting at a left node and a right node. The battery drives current from the top node to the bottom node; the galvanometer bridges the left and right nodes. Each side (R1–R3 and R2–Rx) is just a voltage divider. When the two dividers produce the same voltage at the left and right nodes, no current flows through the galvanometer — the bridge is "balanced."

2. Key formulas

R_1 R_x = R_2 R_3
  • R1, R2, R3: known, adjustable resistors (Ω). Rx: the unknown resistance (Ω).
  • At balance, Rx = R2·R3 / R1 — the unknown is found purely from a ratio of known resistors, not from any absolute voltage or current measurement.
  • Off balance, the galvanometer sees a small bridge voltage Vg ≈ V·[R3/(R1+R3) − Rx/(R2+Rx)] (assuming a high-impedance galvanometer that draws negligible current).

3. What to observe

In the sim. Adjust R1, R2, R3 and Rx and watch the galvanometer needle: it swings toward zero as the bridge approaches balance, and sits still (green) exactly when R1·Rx = R2·R3. The lower plot shows Vg vs Rx, crossing zero at the balance point.

4. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Picture the two arms R1–R3 and R2–Rx as independent voltage dividers fed by the same battery.
Step B. Write the governing relation: R_1 R_x = R_2 R_3
Step C. Change Rx (or R1, R2, R3) and predict whether the galvanometer deflection grows or shrinks before you look.
Step D. Because balance is a null (zero-current) condition, the measurement does not depend on the battery voltage being known precisely — this is why bridge methods are so precise and self-calibrating.
