Spark Discharge

A transient breakdown of a gas gap — modeled with an RC relaxation-oscillator circuit that charges toward breakdown, sparks over, and repeats.

1. Principle

Idea. A spark is a transient electrical breakdown — unlike a glow or arc discharge, it is not self-sustained. The gap breaks down the instant the applied voltage crosses the Paschen breakdown threshold V_b(p·d), a brief high-current channel forms, and the voltage across the gap collapses. If nothing keeps recharging the gap, the discharge stops as quickly as it started. A classic demonstration is a neon-lamp relaxation oscillator: a capacitor C charges through a resistor R from a supply V_supply. The instant the capacitor voltage reaches V_b, the gap sparks over and rapidly discharges the capacitor, after which it starts charging again — producing a repeating sawtooth voltage punctuated by periodic sparks.

2. Key formulas

V_b(pd) = B·(pd) / [ln(A·pd) − ln(ln(1+1/γ))] t = R·C·ln[V_supply / (V_supply − V_b)] f_spark = 1 / t (only if V_supply > V_b)
  • p·d is the product of gas pressure and gap spacing (Paschen's law variable); A, B, γ are teaching constants for air.
  • t is the RC charging time to reach V_b, from the standard exponential charging law V(t) = V_supply·(1 − e^(−t/RC)) solved for t.
  • If V_supply ≤ V_b, the capacitor voltage asymptotically approaches V_supply and never reaches breakdown — the gap never fires.

4. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Start from Paschen's law: as you change pressure or gap spacing, p·d moves along the U-shaped breakdown curve, and V_b changes accordingly.
Step B. Write the RC charging relation: t = R·C·ln[V_supply/(V_supply−V_b)]
Step C. Predict before you look: does increasing R or C speed up or slow down the spark rate? Does raising V_supply toward V_b make sparks more or less frequent?
Step D. Compare a spark (transient, self-extinguishing, high peak current for a brief instant) to a glow discharge (steady, self-sustained, low current density) and an arc discharge (steady, self-sustained, very high current, thermionic-assisted). The differences hinge on whether an external circuit continues to supply current fast enough to sustain the ionized channel.

5. Real-world sparks

Everyday examples. Static discharge (a shock after walking on carpet) is a spark: charge built up over time, then a rapid, transient breakdown across a small air gap once the field exceeds threshold. A spark plug in a gasoline engine works the same way — a coil charges up, and the plug's gap breaks down transiently once per cycle to ignite the fuel-air mixture. Both are single, brief breakdown events rather than continuous discharges.
