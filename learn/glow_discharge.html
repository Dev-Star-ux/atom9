Glow Discharge

A low-pressure gas tube between two DC electrodes: below breakdown, essentially no current flows; above the Paschen breakdown voltage, the gas ionizes and sustains a self-maintained glow with a characteristic luminous structure.

1. Principle

Idea. Free electrons accelerated by the field between cathode and anode ionize gas atoms by collision, producing more electrons and ions in an avalanche. If enough secondary electrons are released from the cathode by ion bombardment (secondary emission, coefficient γ), the discharge becomes self-sustained without an external ionization source — this is breakdown. The breakdown voltage depends on the product of pressure p and electrode spacing d, not on p or d separately: this is Paschen's law.

2. Key formula

V_b = B (pd) / [ln(A·pd) − ln(ln(1+1/γ))]
  • p·d (Torr·cm): pressure × electrode spacing — the single variable Paschen's law depends on.
  • A, B: gas-dependent constants (teaching values for air: A ≈ 15 (cm·Torr)⁻¹, B ≈ 365 V/(cm·Torr)).
  • γ: secondary electron emission coefficient at the cathode (teaching value ≈ 0.01).

3. The Paschen minimum

Why a U-shape? At very low p·d, electrons cross the gap without enough collisions to ionize efficiently, so a very high voltage is needed. At very high p·d, electrons collide too often and lose energy before gaining enough to ionize, so voltage must rise again. Between these extremes lies a minimum breakdown voltage — the Paschen minimum — the easiest condition to strike a discharge.

4. Luminous structure (neon-sign physics)

In the sim. Once V exceeds V_b, the tube lights up in bands from cathode to anode: a thin bright cathode glow, a darker cathode (Crookes) dark space, a broad bright negative glow, a dim Faraday dark space, and a long positive column filling most of the tube. These bands are exactly what you see in a neon sign or fluorescent tube — different zones of ionization, recombination, and excitation along the current path.

5. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Fix pressure p and spacing d; compute p·d and read V_b off the Paschen curve.
Step B. Write the governing relation: V_b = B (pd) / [ln(A·pd) − ln(ln(1+1/γ))]
Step C. Raise the applied voltage V above V_b and watch the tube ignite; change p or d and predict whether V_b rises or falls before you look.
Step D. Compare two different p·d values that give the same V_b (one on each side of the minimum) — Paschen's law says both should break down at the same voltage.
