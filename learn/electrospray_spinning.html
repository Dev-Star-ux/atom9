Experiment guide

Electrostatic Spraying and Spinning

A liquid fed through a nozzle held at high voltage relative to a grounded collector deforms into a Taylor cone above an onset voltage; low-viscosity liquids break into a self-dispersing charged spray, high-viscosity liquids whip into a continuous nanofiber.

1. Principle

Idea. As the voltage on the nozzle rises, the electric field pulls the liquid meniscus into a cone (the Taylor cone). Above the onset voltage a thin jet is drawn from the cone tip. What happens to that jet depends on the liquid's viscosity: a low-viscosity liquid (paints, coatings) cannot sustain a continuous thread — the jet breaks into fine charged droplets that then repel each other by Coulomb repulsion, spreading into a self-dispersing, more uniform spray cone (electrospray). A high-viscosity liquid (a polymer solution) resists breaking up; instead the jet undergoes a bending/whipping instability that stretches and thins it into a long continuous nanofiber, collected as a nonwoven fiber mat (electrospinning).

2. Key formulas

q_R = 8\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \gamma R^3}
  • q_R: maximum (Rayleigh limit) charge a droplet of radius R can hold before Coulomb repulsion overcomes surface tension and the droplet fissions, C.
  • ε₀: vacuum permittivity, 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m. γ: surface tension, N/m. R: droplet radius, m.
  • The onset voltage in the simulator is a simplified teaching estimate (not a precise Taylor-cone formula) that increases with surface tension and nozzle size and increases slowly (logarithmically) with nozzle-to-collector distance.

3. What to observe

In the sim. Below onset voltage the nozzle just drips. Above onset with a low-viscosity liquid, watch fine droplets fan outward as they fall — mutual Coulomb repulsion pushes them apart, giving a more uniform spray than an uncharged nozzle. Switch to a high-viscosity liquid and the same jet instead winds into a whipping, zig-zagging thread that lands as a tangled fiber mat. The bottom plot shows the Rayleigh charge limit q_R rising with droplet radius R — a droplet must fission (split) once its charge would exceed this curve.

4. Applications

Electrospray. Fine, self-dispersing coatings and paint sprays, crop spraying, and inkjet-like fine liquid dispersion — charged droplets repel each other in flight, giving more even coverage than an uncharged spray.
Electrospinning. Continuous polymer nanofibers a few tens to hundreds of nanometers thick, used for air/water filtration media, tissue-engineering scaffolds, and protective textiles.

5. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Start from the physical picture: a charged liquid meniscus balancing electric stress against surface tension.
Step B. Write the governing relation: q_R = 8\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \gamma R^3}
Step C. Change one control at a time (voltage, viscosity mode, surface tension) in the experiment and predict the sign of the change before you look.
Step D. When an AI panel is present, collect data and compare the learned constant with the accepted value.
