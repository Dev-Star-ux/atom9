Experiment guide

Photomultiplier Tube

Incident photons eject photoelectrons at a photocathode; a dynode chain multiplies the electron cloud stage by stage until a huge, easily-measured current arrives at the anode.

1. Principle

Idea. Light striking the photocathode releases photoelectrons via the photoelectric effect — the photocurrent is proportional to the incident photon rate (intensity × quantum efficiency). Each photoelectron is accelerated toward the first dynode and, on impact, knocks out several secondary electrons (yield δ per stage). That larger cloud is accelerated to the next dynode, and the process repeats through the whole chain, multiplying the electron count at every stage before the amplified charge is collected at the anode.

2. Key formulas

G = \delta^n\quad\cdot\quad I_{out} = I_{cathode}\cdot G
  • δ = secondary emission yield per dynode stage (grows with the inter-dynode accelerating voltage).
  • n = number of dynode stages in the chain.
  • G = total gain; I_cathode = photoelectron current leaving the photocathode; I_out = amplified anode current.

3. What to observe

In the sim. The bottom plot shows gain G rising steeply (note the log y-axis) as the number of dynode stages n increases, with a marker at the current n.

4. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Start from the physical picture: photon → photoelectron → cascading secondary emission through the dynode chain → collected anode current.
Step B. Write the governing relation: G = \delta^n\quad\cdot\quad I_{out} = I_{cathode}\cdot G
Step C. Change one control at a time — raise the inter-dynode voltage (δ increases) or add dynode stages (n increases) — and predict how much the gain will jump before you look, since the growth is exponential, not linear.
Step D. Notice why this exponential amplification is what lets a PMT register a single incoming photon as a distinct, measurable current pulse — the basis of photon counting.

5. Applications

Scintillation detectors. A scintillator crystal converts absorbed radiation into a faint flash of light; the PMT behind it amplifies that flash into a countable electrical pulse, the core of most gamma spectrometers and radiation counters.
Low-light imaging. PMTs are used wherever extremely faint light must be detected reliably, such as in fluorescence microscopy, astronomical photometry, and particle-physics Cherenkov detectors.
