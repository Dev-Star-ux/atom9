Meissner Effect

Below T_c a Type-I superconductor actively expels an applied magnetic field from its bulk via persistent surface screening currents — perfect diamagnetism, not just zero resistance.

1. Principle

Idea. A merely perfect conductor (zero resistance, no diamagnetism) would only "freeze" whatever field was already present when it became resistance-free — cooling it in a field would leave that field trapped inside. A real superconductor does something stronger: it actively pushes the field OUT of its interior as it crosses T_c, regardless of the field's history. This active expulsion is the Meissner effect, and it is what makes superconductivity a distinct thermodynamic phase rather than just "infinite conductivity." The field does not vanish abruptly at the surface — it decays smoothly over a characteristic length, the London penetration depth λ_L, as persistent surface screening currents build up to cancel the field inside.

2. Key formulas

B(x) = B_0\,e^{-x/\lambda_L}\quad (T
  • B(x): field magnitude at depth x below the surface. B_0: applied field at the surface. λ_L: London penetration depth (typically tens to hundreds of nm).
  • At x = λ_L, the field has dropped to B_0/e ≈ 37% of its surface value — the standard way to read a penetration depth off a decay curve.
  • Ask which quantity you control in the simulator (temperature, applied field, λ_L, hover height) and which you measure (surface field, decay curve, levitation force).

3. What to observe

In the sim. Below T_c the puck glows and field lines bow outward around it — none penetrate the interior — while a small magnet hovers and gently bobs above it (magnetic levitation from diamagnetic repulsion). Raise T above T_c and the glow disappears, field lines pass straight through, and the magnet settles directly onto the puck: no expulsion, no repulsion, no levitation.

4. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Start from the physical picture: persistent, dissipationless surface currents that arise below T_c and generate a magnetization that exactly cancels the applied field in the bulk.
Step B. Write the governing relation: B(x) = B_0\,e^{-x/\lambda_L}
Step C. Change one control at a time (T, λ_L, B_0, hover height) in the experiment and predict the sign of the change — does the field decay faster or slower, does the magnet float higher or lower — before you look.
Step D. Connect it to real demos: maglev trains use similar repulsion/attraction principles at engineering scale, and "quantum locking" (flux pinning in Type-II superconductors) lets a puck hover rigidly at a fixed height above a magnetic track — a close cousin of the Meissner levitation shown here.
