Thermionic Emission

A heated cathode boils off electrons over its work function barrier; a vacuum-diode I-V curve shows temperature-limited and space-charge-limited regimes.

1. Principle

Idea. Inside a metal, conduction electrons are bound by a potential barrier called the work function W. At room temperature almost none have enough thermal energy to escape. Heat the cathode (filament) hot enough and the high-energy tail of the electron thermal distribution can climb over W and leave the surface — this is thermionic emission, the basis of vacuum tubes, CRTs, and electron guns. The saturated emission current density follows the Richardson-Dushman law.

2. Key formulas

J = A T^2 e^{-W/(k_B T)}
  • J: saturated current density (A/m²) — the maximum an unlimited-supply cathode could emit at temperature T.
  • A: Richardson constant (material-dependent; a generic teaching value ≈ 6.0×10⁵ A/(m²·K²) is used here).
  • T: absolute cathode temperature (K).
  • W: work function (eV, converted to Joules for the exponent), k_B: Boltzmann constant.
  • In an actual vacuum diode, the current that flows also depends on the anode voltage V. At low V, the cloud of electrons near the cathode (space charge) repels further emission and the current is space-charge-limited, rising roughly as V^1.5 (Child-Langmuir law). At high V, every emitted electron is swept away and the current saturates at the Richardson value — temperature-limited.

3. What to observe

In the sim. The filament glows brighter/whiter as T rises, and more electron dots boil off and race to the anode. The bottom plot shows the I-V curve: a rising V^1.5 branch (space-charge-limited) that flattens into a plateau (temperature-limited). The gold dot marks the current operating point, and the readout names which regime is binding.

4. Step-by-step reasoning

Step A. Start from the physical picture: a hot metal surface, a work-function barrier, and a thermal distribution of electron energies.
Step B. Write the governing relation: J = A T^2 e^{-W/(k_B T)}
Step C. Raise T with W fixed and predict the sign and rough size of the change in J before you look — note how sensitive J is to T because W/(k_B T) sits in an exponent. Then raise W with T fixed and see the opposite, strongly suppressing effect.
Step D. Sweep the anode voltage V at fixed T and W and watch the diode current transition from the space-charge-limited branch to the temperature-limited plateau — identify the crossover voltage.

5. History note: the Edison effect

Edison effect. Thomas Edison noticed in 1883 that current could flow from a heated filament to a nearby plate inside a light bulb, even across a vacuum gap — an early observation of thermionic emission. Owen Richardson later derived the T² e^(−W/k_BT) law (Nobel Prize 1928), and the effect became the working principle of vacuum-tube diodes, triodes, and eventually electron guns in CRTs and X-ray tubes.
